Compaction, Not Drought

Every raised bed on the east side wilts by two in the afternoon, and for a season and a
half I assumed it was drought, the soil just couldn't hold water long enough to get the
tomatoes through a hot stretch, so I doubled the mulch and moved the drip line closer to the
stems. It didn't help. Turns out the problem was never moisture at all; it was compaction
three inches down, a hard-packed layer left over from the contractor's foot traffic the year
the beds went in, and no amount of surface watering was ever going to reach roots that
couldn't push past it. I found this out the slow way, with a garden fork and an hour I
didn't plan to spend.

The fix was almost embarrassingly simple. A single broadfork pass, twice, a week apart.
Roots do the rest.

I want to be careful here about what I'm actually claiming. I haven't run this across every
bed in the yard, only the three on the east side, so I can't say compaction explains every
wilting bed anyone will ever have; I can say it explained mine, and that the symptom looked
exactly like drought stress until I put a fork in the ground and felt the difference myself.

Next season I'm building a fourth bed on that same stretch, and I already know the first
thing I'm doing before a single seed goes in.
